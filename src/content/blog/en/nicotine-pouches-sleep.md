Nicotine within four hours of bedtime cost 1.74 percent of sleep efficiency and added six minutes awake during the night, measured by actigraphy across 5,164 nights. Among people with insomnia symptoms it cost 42 minutes of sleep. Caffeine, measured the same way in the same study, showed no significant effect on anything. Nobody has run that study on nicotine pouches specifically.

## What the actigraphy study actually found

The study is [Evening intake of alcohol, caffeine, and nicotine: night-to-night associations with sleep duration and continuity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6802565) by Christine E. Spadola, Na Guo, Dayna A. Johnson and colleagues, published in *Sleep* in 2019 as part of the Jackson Heart Sleep Study.

785 participants wore wrist actigraphy for an average of 6.7 nights each and kept a nightly diary of what they had consumed within four hours of going to bed. That is **5,164 nights** of measured sleep matched to same-day intake, which is what makes it different from asking people how they slept.

| Within 4 hours of bed | Sleep efficiency | Time awake in the night | Sleep duration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nicotine | **−1.74%** (−2.79 to −0.68) | **+6.09 min** (0.82 to 11.35) | −12.97 min (not significant) |
| Alcohol | −0.98% (−1.67 to −0.29) | no association | no association |
| Caffeine | −0.15% (not significant) | +1.13 min (not significant) | −1.15 min (not significant) |

And the number that carries the article: among participants with insomnia symptoms, evening nicotine was associated with **42.47 minutes less sleep** (95% CI −73.37 to −11.56).

## Caffeine lost to nicotine, and that is the surprising part

Almost everyone with a sleep problem audits their coffee first. In this cohort, coffee was not the thing.

Caffeine within four hours of bed produced no significant association with sleep efficiency, wakefulness or duration. Every confidence interval crossed zero. Nicotine produced significant effects on two of the three, and a large one in the group that could least afford it.

That is one cohort, in one population, and it does not mean caffeine is harmless for you personally. It does mean that a person cutting coffee at 4pm while taking a pouch at 10.30pm has probably optimised the wrong variable.

## Your evening pouch is not the only one that counts

The timing advice everyone gives assumes nicotine leaves your system between doses. It mostly does not.

In [Nicotine Chemistry, Metabolism, Kinetics and Biomarkers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2953858/), Neal L. Benowitz, Janne Hukkanen and Peyton Jacob III report a plasma half-life averaging **about two hours** — and then the consequence of that, which is the part that gets left out:

> "Based on a half-life of 2 h for nicotine, one would predict accumulation over 6–8 h of regular smoking."

Regular use through the day builds a level that persists across all 24 hours. Measured by urinary excretion, which picks up lower concentrations, the terminal half-life averages **11 hours**.

So the afternoon matters. Someone who uses pouches steadily from lunchtime onwards arrives at bedtime on an accumulated level, not on the residue of one pouch. A four-hour gap helps, and it is not the same as arriving at zero.

## The 3am wake-up is the withdrawal, not the nicotine

This is the mechanism people rarely have described to them, and it explains the pattern that makes no sense otherwise: falling asleep fine and waking at three.

With a two-hour half-life, someone who takes a last pouch at 10pm is in meaningful withdrawal by the small hours. The narrative review [Effects of Nicotine on the Central Nervous System and Sleep Quality](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10733894/) by Nihaal Singh, Anil Wanjari and Arya Harshyt Sinha (*Cureus*, 2023) names this directly as "nocturnal sleep-disturbing nicotine craving".

Insomnia is also one of the seven nicotine withdrawal symptoms defined in the DSM-5. The full list, and how it runs over the first weeks after stopping, is on the [quit snus](/quit-snus) page.

Which leaves an uncomfortable symmetry: using nicotine in the evening disturbs the first half of the night, and running out of it disturbs the second.

## Where the evidence gets weaker, honestly

Two things pull against the story above, and leaving them out would make this page the same as every other page on the topic.

**The randomised evidence is null.** A secondary analysis of the ESTxENDS trial by Tamara Scharf and colleagues, published in *Sleep* in February 2026, followed 831 people randomised to free e-cigarettes plus cessation counselling or counselling alone. At six months there was **no significant difference in overall sleep quality** on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (adjusted slope −0.20, p = 0.256). Adding a nicotine product did not measurably change how people slept.

**The observational evidence has a publication-bias signal.** A systematic review by Huma Sulthana and colleagues in *Frontiers in Public Health* (29 August 2025) pooled 14 cross-sectional studies and found e-cigarette users at higher odds of short sleep, **OR 1.38 (95% CI 1.24 to 1.55)**. The authors flag that every included study was cross-sectional, so causality is not established, and Egger's test for publication bias came out at p = 0.03.

The honest reading: nicotine close to bedtime has a measured, same-night effect. Whether being a nicotine user makes you a worse sleeper in general is much less settled than the internet suggests.

## Nobody has studied pouches and sleep

Not one trial. The nearest evidence is the nicotine research above, plus what is known about how pouches deliver it.

That gap matters more than it sounds, because pouches differ from cigarettes in exactly the way that would affect sleep: they are used in long, quiet stretches rather than in discrete breaks, and a pouch can sit in for 30 to 60 minutes. An evening of three cigarettes and an evening of three pouches are not the same exposure curve, and nobody has measured which one costs more sleep.

## What to change, in order

1. **Move the last pouch four hours before bed.** This is the only interval the research tested, so it is the one to start with. Not three, not "a while before".
2. **Then look at the afternoon count**, because of the accumulation above. If the last one is at 6pm but there were seven between noon and then, the four-hour rule is doing less than it appears.
3. **Then, and only then, the strength.** Nicotine per pouch varies roughly tenfold across the market, which is covered in [how strong a nicotine pouch really is](/blog/nicotine-pouch-strength). Change one thing at a time or you cannot read the result.
4. **Give it two weeks before judging.** Night-to-night sleep is noisy. A single good or bad night tells you nothing.
