The short answer: controlled trials do not support a meaningful performance benefit from nicotine, but pouches are widespread in professional sport anyway, and the people using them are mostly dependent. Those two facts sit uncomfortably next to each other, and they are the whole story.

## Is nicotine banned in sport?

No. Nicotine is **not** on the World Anti-Doping Agency's Prohibited List. It sits on WADA's [Monitoring Program](https://www.wada-ama.org/en/resources/monitoring-program), where in-competition use is tracked as a stimulant to detect patterns of misuse. Substances on that programme are not prohibited, using them is not an anti-doping rule violation, and there is no sanction attached.

That is worth stating plainly, because "is snus banned" is one of the most common questions in this space and the answer has been stable for over a decade. Monitoring exists precisely so that WADA has the data if a ban ever becomes justified.

## What the performance research actually found

The honest summary of the exercise literature is: not much happens.

Across roughly ten studies looking at maximal strength, submaximal and maximal endurance, and high-intensity exercise, **seven found no change in performance**, two found positive effects, and one found a negative effect. That distribution is what "no reliable effect" looks like in practice. When effects do appear, they are small and inconsistent in direction.

A few specifics worth knowing:

- One trial found cyclists on nicotine patches reached exhaustion later than those without. That is the strongest single result in favour, and it is one study.
- Another found nicotine raised heart rate during exercise **without** improving performance, which is the worst of both.
- Cognitive and fine motor effects are the more consistent finding. A study in archery found faster processing and better hand-eye accuracy against placebo, though the benefit is typically followed by a dip in focus.

So if there is anything real here, it is short-term attention, not physical output. And attention gains from a substance you are dependent on are hard to separate from simply relieving your own withdrawal.

## One in five professionals, and most of them dependent

This is the part that changes the picture. The Professional Footballers' Association and [Loughborough University](https://www.lboro.ac.uk/news-events/news/2024/may/snus-study-findings-published/) surveyed 628 men's and 51 women's professional players in England, plus interviews with 16 club performance and medical staff. The results were published in the *Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport*.

| Finding | Men's game | Women's game |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Currently use snus or nicotine pouches | 18% | 22% |
| Of users, reported elements of nicotine dependence | 53% | 73% |

Roughly **one in five** professional footballers uses it. Of those, **the majority show signs of dependence**. Players describe the appeal as focus and as relaxation after training, not as a performance aid, which lines up with what the trials found.

## The cost that does not show up in a performance test

Nicotine is a stimulant. Disrupted sleep and restlessness appear both as effects of use and as withdrawal symptoms when you stop. For anyone training seriously, that is the most relevant line in the whole discussion, because sleep is where adaptation actually happens. A supplement with no proven performance benefit that costs you sleep quality is a bad trade even before you get to dependence.

The second cost is the dependence itself. A habit built around training sessions is a habit anchored to a schedule you repeat several times a week, which is exactly the structure that makes it hard to break later.
