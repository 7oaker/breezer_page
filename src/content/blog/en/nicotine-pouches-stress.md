Nicotine pouches relieve the stress that not having one produces. Across 102 studies and more than 169,500 people, anxiety, depression and stress all improved after quitting rather than getting worse. The relief you feel when the pouch goes in is real and measurable. What it measures is the gap that opened since the last one.

## What quitting does to anxiety and stress

The largest review of this question is [Smoking cessation for improving mental health](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013522_does-stopping-smoking-improve-mental-health), by Gemma M. J. Taylor, Nicola Lindson, Amanda Farley and colleagues, published by Cochrane on 9 March 2021. It covers **102 studies and over 169,500 participants**.

Every outcome moved in favour of quitting:

| Outcome | Effect (SMD) | Studies | Participants |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anxiety | **−0.28** | 15 | 3,141 |
| Mixed anxiety and depression | **−0.31** | 8 | 2,829 |
| Depression | −0.30 | 34 | 7,156 |
| Stress | −0.19 | 4 | 1,792 |
| Positive affect | +0.22 | 13 | 4,880 |
| Psychological quality of life | +0.11 | 19 | 18,034 |

The certainty ratings vary and are worth stating: moderate for mixed anxiety and depression, low for anxiety, very low for depression. So the size of the effect is uncertain. The direction is not, and the authors put it plainly: mental health does not worsen as a result of quitting smoking.

## The comparison that makes the size legible

An SMD of −0.28 sounds like nothing until you put it next to something familiar.

The earlier meta-analysis by the same research group, [Change in mental health after smoking cessation](https://www.bmj.com/content/348/bmj.g1151) by Gemma Taylor, Ann McNeill, Alan Girling, Amanda Farley, Nicola Lindson-Hawley and Paul Aveyard, published in the *BMJ* on 13 February 2014, pooled 26 studies and found:

- **Anxiety −0.37** (95% CI −0.70 to −0.03)
- **Stress −0.27** (−0.40 to −0.13)
- **Mixed anxiety and depression −0.31** (−0.47 to −0.14)
- **Positive affect +0.40** (0.09 to 0.71)

Their conclusion contains the sentence worth remembering: "The effect sizes are equal or larger than those of antidepressant treatment for mood and anxiety disorders."

The authors also report that the benefit was as large for people with a psychiatric diagnosis as for people without one, which is the opposite of the assumption that usually stops that group being offered help to quit.

## So why does it feel like relief

Because it is relief. Just not from the stress you think.

Andy C. Parrott set out the argument in [Does Cigarette Smoking Cause Stress?](https://www.ovid.com/journals/ampsy/abstract/00000487-199910000-00002~does-cigarette-smoking-cause-stress) in *American Psychologist* in October 1999. The apparent relaxant effect reflects the reversal of the tension and irritability that develop as nicotine levels fall. Dependent users need nicotine to feel normal, and the moods they describe follow that cycle: normal during use, worsening between doses.

Which means the pouch is not moving you above baseline. It is returning you to it, from a dip that the previous pouch created.

That is why the relief is genuine, immediate and completely convincing, and why it never accumulates into a less stressed life. Every dose pays back a debt it just took out.

## The measured stress and the felt stress point in opposite directions

Parrott's analysis lines up three observations that are hard to reconcile with nicotine as a stress reliever.

**Adult smokers report slightly higher stress than non-smokers.** Not lower, which is what a working stress medicine would produce.

**Adolescents report rising stress as their use becomes regular.** The direction of travel is the wrong way for a calming agent, and it tracks the development of dependence.

**Stopping is followed by less stress.** Which is the finding the two meta-analyses above quantify.

None of that requires believing the relief is imaginary. It requires separating the minute after the pouch from the month.

## The first days go the other way, and that is expected

This is the part the reassuring version of this article leaves out.

Anxiety, irritability, restlessness and difficulty concentrating are recognised nicotine withdrawal symptoms, and withdrawal peaks around day three. Anyone stopping should expect the mental-health picture to get worse before the numbers above start applying, because those numbers are measured at follow-up, weeks and months later.

The two claims are not in conflict. One describes day three, the other describes month three. Confusing them is how "I tried and my anxiety got much worse" becomes evidence that quitting is bad for you.

The full withdrawal timeline, symptom by symptom, is on the [quit snus](/quit-snus) page.

## A pouch is a stimulant, not a sedative

Whatever the calm is, it is not the body slowing down.

In a crossover trial at LMU Munich published in [*Frontiers in Pharmacology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2024.1392027/full) in May 2024, a 30 mg pouch raised heart rate by about **25 beats per minute**, matching a cigarette, and raised measures of arterial stiffness. A 6 mg pouch did neither.

So the same product, depending on the can, is either a mild dose or a cardiovascular event comparable to smoking. Reading that number is covered in [how strong a nicotine pouch really is](/blog/nicotine-pouch-strength).

## None of this was measured on pouches

Every figure on this page comes from research on smoking. That gap is not a technicality.

The entire randomised evidence base on nicotine pouches amounts to a few hundred participants over a few weeks, and none of those trials measured stress, anxiety or mood as an outcome. What that evidence base looks like in full is in [nicotine pouch side effects, ranked by the evidence](/blog/nicotine-pouch-side-effects).

What transfers reasonably: pouches deliver the same drug, on a cycle of peaks and troughs, to the same receptors. What does not transfer automatically: the size of any of these numbers.

## If stress is the reason you use them

Then stress is the thing to work on, and the pouch is currently occupying the slot where that work would go.

1. **Find out what your actual cycle is.** Not how many per day, but when the gaps happen. The pouches that feel most necessary cluster around the same few moments, and those moments are the information.
2. **Get the support with evidence behind it.** Behavioural counselling has the strongest evidence of anything in this field. In Austria the [Rauchfrei Telefon](https://rauchfrei.at/) on **0800 810 013** is free, anonymous and explicitly product-independent, so pouches are covered.
3. **Expect the first week to contradict everything above.** Plan for it rather than treating it as a verdict.
4. **Do not change psychiatric medication around a quit attempt without your doctor.** Quitting can alter how some medicines are metabolised, and that is a conversation for a prescriber, not a website.
